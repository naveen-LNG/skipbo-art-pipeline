~~~~text
Reproduce the finished art style of the attached style reference images as exactly as possible. They define the finish, the shading, the colour and the shape language, and nothing else is taken from them: not their subjects, not their characters, not their poses and not their compositions.

MEDIUM: A finished colour illustration for a bright casual mobile game. It carries two treatments at once. The setting, meaning the sky, the ground, the water, the distance and the planting, is built from clean simplified masses, each softly shaded across its whole body. The props and the characters that stand in the scene are cleaner, more saturated and more sharply edged against what is behind them, so that they read forward. There is no line art anywhere: no outlines, no contours, no ink, no drawn edges and no dark strokes around anything, and shapes separate from one another only by their own colour and by the shadow between them.

SKY, SEA AND CLOUDS: The sky is one clean vertical gradient, deeper and more saturated at the top and lighter towards the horizon, and it holds nothing but clouds. The clouds are large soft rounded masses built from a few big overlapping lobes, bright white on top and shaded a little cooler and deeper underneath, and each lobe is modelled as a rounded volume in its own right so the cloud has real depth and never reads as a flat cut-out silhouette. They sit low near the horizon and read as solid shapes rather than as mist or haze. The sea is horizontal bands of clean saturated colour: the deepest and most saturated at the horizon, stepping to a brighter turquoise, then to a pale mint at the shore, and where it meets the land the foam is drawn as two or three soft overlapping curves of slightly different weight and length, thinning out towards the sides, rather than as one even mechanical line. If a sun is in the scene it is one clean disc with a soft even glow immediately around it and nothing more: no rays, no streaks, no lens flare, no wide wash of haze across the sky, and no bleaching of the colours near it.

THE SETTING: Ground, water, hills, distant land and planting are each one simplified mass with a soft rounded silhouette, softly shaded across its body, with a slightly deeper tone along its lower edge where it meets what is in front. Sand and grass may carry a very gentle broad mottling of a slightly different tone, nothing more. Masses overlap one another in clear steps and each step reads as a distinct plane. Foliage is built from rounded lobed canopies with a deeper underside. Water is two or three soft bands of clean colour with a few crisp white foam curves. Distance is paler, cooler and quieter, its shapes reduced further, but it stays perfectly sharp.

THE PROPS AND CHARACTERS: Every object and every character is shaded with soft broad volume: one wide passage of light where the light falls, one wide passage of shade where the form turns away, and a smooth gentle transition between them running across the whole body of the object, plus one broad soft highlight where the light strikes most directly. That is the whole of the shading: two wide zones and one highlight, with no further modelling, no small facets and no detail lighting. Each object casts one shadow onto the ground, and that shadow is darkest and tightest exactly where the object touches the ground, then softens and fades as it travels away from that contact until it loses its edge altogether at the far end. It is a deeper tone of the ground itself and never a grey.

FLAT DIVISIONS: Wherever two colours meet inside a single object they meet as flat areas along one clean edge and nothing happens at that edge. The wedges of a parasol, the sectors of a beach ball, the stripes of a towel, the band of a hat: each is a flat field of colour, and the join carries no bevel, no ridge, no raised lip, no inner shadow, no glow and no strip of highlight. The soft shading passes straight over those divisions as though they were printed on the surface, because that is what they are. This holds just as much on a curved body: on a ball or a dome the coloured sectors are flat fields that simply follow the curve, and none of them is given a shading, a highlight or an edge of its own. The one soft volume shading of the whole ball runs across every sector alike, so the ball reads as one round form printed with colour rather than as separate panels assembled together.

RIM LIGHT: A few rim lights are present and they are wanted, but only a few: on two or three forms in the whole picture, those standing directly against the scene's own light, a narrow band of lighter colour runs along the lit edge. Each one is light and subtle, a step up from the local colour rather than a white line, and it covers only part of that edge. Its own edge is clean but not razor sharp: the band fades away at both of its ends and melts a little into the local colour along its inner side, so it reads as light catching a soft form and never as a hard bright sliver stuck onto it. Most objects carry none at all, and a rim light never runs around a whole outline and never along a division inside an object.

SURFACE: No surface is described by fine repeated detail. Wood has no grain lines, sand has no speckle, leaves have no vein networks and no serrated edges, fabric has no weave and no fibres, basketwork has no visible weaving and no woven pattern, stone has no pitting, and a flower is a few plain rounded petals with no veining and no ruffled edge. Where a material must be told apart it is told apart by its colour and by a very few large clean marks: three plank joints across a deck, one dark stripe down a leaf, two bright curves on the water.

FORM LANGUAGE: Everything is built from big generous rounded volumes with a bold readable silhouette. Corners are softened, edges have thickness, and nothing is thin, spindly, angular or fragile. Objects are simplified into a few large clear masses: a bush is a cluster of soft domes, a tree is a stack of rounded canopies on a curved trunk, a rock is a smooth pebble. A flower is a few plain rounded petals lying flat against the plant, each one a simple shape with no thickness of its own: no extruded edge, no raised lip, no side wall and no rim running round it, so a petal never looks pressed out of the surface. Nothing is drawn thin: a basket, a hat brim, a surfboard, a plank, a pole and a stem are all given real thickness and a generous chunky mass, noticeably thicker than they would be in life, because a thin form reads as weak and fragile at this size. Everything is drawn larger and bolder than life and reads instantly from its silhouette alone.

THEME COLOURS: The scene is defined by exactly three theme colours, named in the scene description with their hue, saturation and brightness: a dominant, a second that supports it, and a third that is lighter and paler. Those three carry the picture and everything else in it is a tone or a close neighbour of one of them.

COLOUR: Bright, vivid and strongly saturated, at the strength of the house palette itself: most of the picture sits between fifty five and eighty five percent saturation with brightness above ninety, and the colours are the frank clean colours of that palette rather than softened versions of them. Never dusty, muddy, greyed, milky, chalky, pastel or washed out. The whole image reads light with no dark passages, even at night, where the darkness stays a deep clean blue rather than a black. The dominant theme colour covers about half the coloured surface, so the scene can be named by it, and a complementary colour of the opposite temperature runs through the picture covering between a tenth and a third without ever rivalling it. Depth is built by using the dominant across its tonal range, paler and quieter behind, stronger and cleaner in front, never by darkening. Colour is spread, never piled up: each of the three theme colours appears in at least three separated places across the frame, near the top, in the middle and low down, so the eye finds it again as it travels. No colour is allowed to collect in one region of the picture, and the props of a group are not all given the same colour: they are shared out among the three theme colours so the group reads as varied rather than as one mass of a single hue.

DEPTH: Three clear bands, each a distinct step. A foreground band along the bottom, cropped by the frame, largest in scale. A middle band carrying the subject, the crispest and most saturated part of the picture. A background band behind, paler and simpler, its shapes reduced further but still perfectly sharp. Distance is read as a step down in saturation and a step up in lightness, never as blur, haze or fog.

AVOID: Outlines, contours, ink lines and dark strokes around shapes. A three dimensional rendered look: inflated plastic forms, glossy moulded surfaces, specular blobs, inner glow, inner shadow, and bevelled or highlighted edges where two colours meet. A flat vector, clip-art or sticker look, and forms left unshaded. A painterly or brushy look, visible brush strokes, grain and noise. Photographic rendering, depth of field, blur, bloom, lens flare and fog. Fine repeated texture. Rim light on every object. A busy sky. Dark passages and heavy contrast. Text, lettering, logos and watermarks.

FRAME AND EYE LEVEL: A tall vertical frame in nine by sixteen. The camera sits low, close to the ground, well below standing height, so the viewer looks across the scene rather than down onto it and the ground plane opens out towards the bottom of the frame. The horizon sits low, at about the middle of the height or a little under it, and everything above it is sky or distance. This low eye level is what makes a scene of this kind feel like a place you are standing in, and it is different from a scene looked down upon.

THE THREE BANDS: A foreground band runs across the bottom of the frame and is cut by the left and right edges: a deck, a path, a low wall, a shoreline or a stretch of ground, seen almost edge on because the camera is low, in the strongest colour of the picture. A middle band above it holds the subject of the scene and everything that matters, on open ground with room around it. A background band closes the picture with the distance and the sky, paler and quieter than everything in front.

SIDE FRAMING: A tall vertical element stands at the left edge and another at the right edge, running most of the height and cropped by the frame: a tree trunk with its canopy overhead, a post, a column, a hanging plant, a bank of foliage or the corner of a building. They frame the middle of the picture, they are part of the setting and never part of the action, and they carry no element of the progression and no character. They are what fills the outer margins where the interface sits.

SAFE AREAS: The painting fills the tall vertical frame completely, edge to edge, with the setting running out to all four edges. The safe area is the band from thirty seven to seventy four percent of the height across from twelve to eighty eight percent of the width, plus above it only the central column between twenty eight and seventy two percent of the width. An object that matters may extend outside it and be cropped by the frame, and often should, but the part that identifies it must sit inside the safe area so it is recognised there even when the interface covers the rest. Nothing that matters is readable only in the bottom quarter, only in the upper corner blocks or only in the outer margins.

GROUPING: The props and characters gather into one clear group in the middle band, close enough to read as one arrangement, with clear space around it so its silhouette stays legible. They are never spread evenly across the width and never scattered into the corners.

LENS AND PERSPECTIVE: The camera is rectilinear and the frame carries no lens distortion. Straight lines stay straight from one edge of the frame to the other and verticals stay vertical: a fence rail, a post, a plank, a horizon and a tree trunk do not bow, curve or splay towards the corners. There is no fisheye, no barrel distortion, no wide angle bulge, no curved horizon and no vignette.
~~~~
